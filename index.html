About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Economics at Simon Fraser University. My research interests lie in banking, financial stability, and international macroeconomics, with a particular focus on how supervisory communication, sovereign risk, and digital state capacity shape financial markets and fiscal outcomes. I also conduct research on how wildfire risk information influences household preparedness using a randomized controlled trial (RCT).

Previously, I worked as a Senior Analyst in the Finance and Risk Management Department at the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC) and as a Data Analytics Consultant and Training Specialist for Pacific Economic Development Canada.

I hold an M.A. in Economics from Simon Fraser University and a B.A. in Economics from the University of Ghana, where I was recognized as the best student in Economics in both 2018 and 2019. During my undergraduate studies, I also participated in an exchange program at the University of Sussex.

Job Market Paper

Beyond the Fed: The Information Content of Bank Supervisory Communication

This paper examines whether public bank supervisory communication contains information relevant to financial markets, bank funding, and financial stability. Using 1,388 speeches, testimonies, and statements issued by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) between 1978 and 2025, I construct a novel supervisory sentiment index and isolate innovations conditional on predetermined financial market information. FDIC communication releases are associated with higher bank and broader market returns, with stronger responses among bank equities. Favorable supervisory sentiment innovations are followed by higher bank excess returns, particularly on the second trading day, but are not significantly associated with S&P 500 returns. These findings are robust to alternative specifications and controls for monetary policy shocks and broader market movements. At the quarterly frequency, favorable innovations are associated with increases in total and core deposits and reductions in deposit interest expenses, with more pronounced funding responses among large banks. Bank level estimates further reveal larger declines in deposit rates among large banks. Together, these funding responses are consistent with an information driven expansion in deposit supply. Favorable innovations are also associated with a significantly stronger response of uninsured relative to insured deposits at financially stronger banks, consistent with risk sensitive depositor allocation. Finally, favorable supervisory innovations are associated with a statistically significant but economically modest reduction in subsequent bank failure odds. Overall, the findings highlight the informational role of public bank supervisory communication in bank valuations, deposit funding, and financial stability.

Digital Government and Sovereign Default Risk: Tax Enforcement, Revenue Diversion, and Fiscal Capacity

Working Paper, 2026

This paper analyzes how government adoption of digital technologies affects sovereign default risk through tax enforcement and revenue diversion. Using panel data for 46 economies observed in 2014 and 2016, I find that higher digital adoption is associated with lower sovereign spreads and higher government tax revenue, conditional on institutional quality and control of corruption. I construct a novel database of digital fiscal reforms by applying natural language processing to Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA) assessments and related policy documents. Event study and staggered difference in differences estimates with entropy balanced weights provide suggestive evidence of increases in tax revenue following reform implementation. To rationalize these findings, I develop a sovereign default model in which tax administrators can divert revenue, and digital capacity increases the expected cost of diversion by raising the probability of detection. Calibrated using the simulated method of moments, the model shows how digital investment strengthens fiscal capacity, improves sovereign repayment incentives, lowers equilibrium spreads, and expands borrowing capacity. At the ergodic mean debt level, digital investment generates a consumption equivalent welfare gain of 20.6% under the benchmark assumption of no initial enforcement capacity, declining to 5.3% under modest pre-existing enforcement capacity. The baseline welfare gain arises predominantly from reduced tax revenue diversion. Welfare gains are modestly larger in more indebted economies but diminish sharply as pre-existing enforcement capacity increases.

Gold as Insurance: Sovereign Default Risk and Central Bank Reserve Reallocation

Working Paper, 2026

This paper examines how sovereign default risk shapes the composition of central bank reserves. Using an annual panel of 52 economies from 1997–2020, I employ four complementary empirical approaches and draw on a newly compiled country-level dataset on the currency composition of official reserves. Higher sovereign default risk, measured by the Emerging Market Bond Index (EMBI), is associated with an initial increase in the gold share of reserves that partially reverses in subsequent years. This relationship remains robust after accounting for reserve currency composition, gold prices, and international sanctions. The initial increase in the gold share is accompanied by a decline in non-gold reserve holdings, consistent with a shift in reserve composition toward gold. Event-study estimates show that the gold share rises following sovereign default crises. Similar patterns emerge around debt restructuring episodes, particularly at the onset of private creditor negotiations. Further analysis provides suggestive evidence of two transmission channels: weakening domestic equity markets and deteriorating reserve adequacy. Together, these findings underscore the importance of country-specific sovereign default risk in explaining central bank reserve composition and provide new evidence consistent with the precautionary role of gold in official reserve management.
